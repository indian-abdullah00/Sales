# Transactional and Retail Sales Classics: Girard, Ziglar, Belfort

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Type** | Books, methods |
| **Author / Origin** | Joe Girard (car sales); Zig Ziglar (sales trainer); Jordan Belfort (Straight Line) |
| **Field** | Consumer, retail and high-pressure sales |
| **Evidence tier** | **B** (Girard), **C** (Ziglar), **D: study, don't copy** (Belfort) |
| **Originals** | Joe Girard, *How to Sell Anything to Anybody* (1977) · Zig Ziglar, *Secrets of Closing the Sale* (1984) · Jordan Belfort, *Way of the Wolf* (2017) |

## Why it made the cut
To round out the picture. These represent the **one-call, transactional** end of sales, which SPIN's research showed behaves differently from complex software sales. Knowing them helps you (a) recognize which tactics *don't* transfer to B2B and (b) borrow the ones that do (follow-up discipline, enthusiasm, conviction).

---

## Joe Girard: the world's greatest retail salesman (Tier B)
- Recognized by **Guinness World Records** for selling **13,001 cars** at a Chevrolet dealership between 1963 and 1977, all retail, one at a time.
- **The Law of 250:** everyone knows about 250 people who'd come to their wedding or funeral. Treat one customer badly and you could lose 250 more.
- **Relentless follow-up:** he sent every customer a card every month ("I like you").

**What transfers to SaaS:** retention and referrals. Every customer is a reference network. Customer success *is* sales.

---

## Zig Ziglar: enthusiasm and closes (Tier C)
- "You can have everything in life you want, if you will just help enough other people get what they want."
- Catalogued many closing techniques: the assumptive close, the alternate-choice close ("Tuesday or Thursday?"), the "Ben Franklin" close (list pros vs. cons).

**What transfers:** the alternate-choice question for *scheduling next steps* ("Does Tuesday or Thursday work for the security review?").
**What doesn't:** Rackham's research showed that classic closes **reduce** success with professional buyers in large deals.

---

## Jordan Belfort: the Straight Line (Tier D, study, don't copy)
- **The Three Tens:** the prospect must be certain (10/10) about the **product**, the **salesperson**, and the **company**, plus a low "action threshold."
- **Tonality and body language** as persuasion tools.
- **Looping:** return to the close again and again after each objection.

**Why Tier D:** Belfort was convicted of securities fraud. The Straight Line was built for high-pressure phone sales of penny stocks. Repeated looping and pressure tactics **damage trust in B2B software**, where the buyer has a committee, does diligence and renews every year.
**What *is* worth noticing:** the "Three Tens" is a useful diagnostic for *why a deal is stuck*. Is the buyer unsure about the product, about you, or about your company?

---

## Comparison: transactional vs. complex software sales
| Dimension | Transactional (Girard / Belfort) | Complex software (SPIN / MEDDPICC) |
|---|---|---|
| Sales cycle | One call | Weeks to months |
| Decision maker | One person | A committee (6–10 people) |
| Closing technique | Strong close | Aim for Advances, mutual plans |
| Relationship after sale | Optional | Essential (renewal, expansion) |
| Biggest risk | Losing the moment | "No decision" ([JOLT](../01-research-backed/jolt-effect-indecision.md)) |
