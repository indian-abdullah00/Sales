## Why it made the cut
It's probably the most influential B2B sales narrative of the last decade. It showed that the best software pitches **don't start with the product, or even the customer's pain. They start with a change in the world.**

## The five-part structure (Raskin)
| # | Step | What Zuora did |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Name a big, relevant change in the world** | "The Subscription Economy": the world is moving from owning products to subscribing to services. |
| 2 | **Show there will be winners and losers** | Companies that adapt to subscriptions (Netflix, Salesforce) win. Companies that don't (Blockbuster) lose. |
| 3 | **Tease the Promised Land** | Not "have our software" but a desirable future state: *a business with subscribers, recurring revenue and lasting relationships.* |
| 4 | **Introduce features as "magic gifts"** | Each capability is a tool for getting past a specific obstacle on the way to the Promised Land (pricing agility, billing for usage, subscriber metrics). |
| 5 | **Present evidence** | Customer stories that prove you can make the story come true. |

## Key dialogue: the narrative, spoken *(illustrative paraphrase of the structure, not Zuora's deck text)*
> **(1) Change:** "Something fundamental has shifted. Customers no longer want to own products. They want outcomes, delivered as a service. You can see it everywhere: music, cars, software, even razors."
>
> **(2) Winners & losers:** "The companies that build direct, ongoing relationships with subscribers are growing several times faster. The ones still selling one-off transactions are watching their customers walk away to someone who will."
>
> **(3) Promised Land:** "The winners have one thing in common: they know every subscriber, they can change pricing in a day, and they grow revenue from the customers they already have."
>
> **(4) Magic gifts:** "Getting there is hard, because your billing system was built for one-time sales. That's what we fix. Here's how you launch a new pricing plan in an afternoon instead of a quarter..."
>
> **(5) Evidence:** "Here's how [customer] moved from licenses to subscriptions and..."

## Why it works (the psychology)
- **The enemy is the change, not the buyer.** The buyer doesn't have to admit they were wrong, only that the world moved.
- **Winners/losers creates urgency without hype.** It raises the stakes of doing nothing (see [JOLT](../01-research-backed/jolt-effect-indecision.md) on status-quo bias).
- **The Promised Land is something to aim for, not a product.** It can't be compared on a spec sheet.

## How to apply it to your product
1. Write one sentence: "**The world has shifted from ___ to ___.**"
2. Name who's winning and who's losing because of it.
3. Describe the Promised Land without mentioning your product.
4. List 3–5 obstacles to getting there, and map one feature to each.
5. Collect one proof point per obstacle.

## Watch-outs
- Raskin himself warns that sophisticated buyers have now seen this structure many times. Use it as a **framework, not a template**.
- The "change" has to be real and specific to the buyer's world. "AI is changing everything" is too generic to carry a pitch.
