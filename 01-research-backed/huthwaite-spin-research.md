| **Year** | 1970s research; book 1988 |
| **Field** | Complex / B2B sales (all industries) |

## Why it made the cut
It's the original large-scale behavioral study of selling: **35,000+ sales calls observed** over about 12 years in more than 20 countries, funded by major corporations. It overturned the "classic" closing techniques of its day, and most modern discovery methods descend from it.

## Core findings
1. **Small sales and large sales are different games.** Tactics that work in a one-call, low-price sale (pushy closes, early feature pitching) *hurt* results in large, multi-call sales.
2. **Closing techniques backfire in big deals.** The more often reps used classic closes with professional buyers, the *less* likely the sale.
3. **Questions beat statements.** Successful calls had far more questions, and specifically a *sequence* of question types:
   - **S**ituation: facts about the current state (use sparingly; buyers find too many of them boring)
   - **P**roblem: difficulties and dissatisfactions
   - **I**mplication: the consequences of those problems (**the strongest predictor of success**)
   - **N**eed-payoff: get the buyer to say the value of solving the problem
4. **Features vs. Advantages vs. Benefits.** Real "benefits" are only the ones that meet an *explicit need the buyer has stated*. Pitching benefits before the need has been made explicit doesn't move big deals.
5. **Objections are mostly created by the seller**, by pitching too early. Good discovery prevents them.
6. **The goal of a call isn't the order; it's an "Advance"**: an agreed action that moves the deal forward (a meeting with the CFO, a pilot). A "Continuation" ("send me some info") is not progress.

## Key dialogue / scripts

*(Illustrative SPIN sequence for a SaaS security product)*

| Type | Example question |
|---|---|
| Situation | "How are employee laptops managed today: one tool or several?" |
| Problem | "How confident are you that every device is patched within a week of a critical CVE?" |
| Implication | "If one unpatched device were breached, what would that mean for your SOC 2 renewal? For the enterprise deals in your pipeline that require it?" |
| Implication | "How many engineering hours go into chasing patch status by hand each month?" |
| Need-payoff | "If you could see every device's patch status in one view and auto-remediate, what would that free your team up to do?" |

> The buyer, not the seller, should say the value out loud. Need-payoff questions make that happen.

## How to apply it
1. Cut down on situation questions. Research the basics before the call.
2. For every problem you uncover, ask **two or three implication questions** before pitching anything.
3. End each call with an **Advance**, a specific next commitment, rather than a vague follow-up.

## Watch-outs
- In the hands of weak reps, implication questions can feel manipulative ("What if your house burned down?"). Keep them grounded in the buyer's real situation.
- The research was done before the internet. Buyers now arrive better informed, which is the gap *Challenger* addresses.
